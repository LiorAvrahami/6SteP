# Skip Spotify, embed YouTube, welcome dancers' files

**A web version of the app can play famous recordings without distributing them in two ways: through YouTube's embedded player, kept visible on the page, and from the dancer's own audio files. It cannot do so through Spotify.** A "virtual browser" logged in to the dancer's Spotify account fails on every count: Spotify refuses to let its web player sit inside another page, a browser run on a server breaks Spotify's user rules and re-sends licensed music from the developer's machine, and Spotify's official tools serve **at most five named users** for an individual developer, under rules that forbid what this app does (pictures synchronised to recordings, other sound over the music, changed tempo). YouTube's player is free for every visitor and gives the app's own buttons play, pause, seek and speed in 5% steps; it is allowed while the player stays visible and uncovered, and practical if a timing test on phones passes. The dancer's own files are the only way to play a recording of the dancer's choice that meets every requirement, including exact position, free tempo change at the same pitch and lowering the music under the voice on iPhone, for the minority of dancers who own files. A third mode, tapping along to music playing in Spotify or any other app, needs nobody's permission but gives up the app's control of the song. Apple Music is obtainable for US$99 a year, yet its licence says its music "cannot be synchronized with any other content", so it should wait for a written answer from Apple; TIDAL, Deezer and Amazon Music do not give outside developers full songs. **Recommended path:** one beat-grid format with interchangeable sound sources, built in this order: built-in, properly licensed tracks as the default (their missing credits need fixing now), "open my own file", a YouTube mode for a curated list of recordings, then tap-along. The famous swing-era recordings themselves cannot ship inside a worldwide app before 2036 to 2046. No rulebook examined mentions donation buttons; the cautious design keeps the button on its own page and ties no feature to it. Two cautions apply throughout: most of the services' rule pages were seen only as search-result excerpts, and nothing was tested on a phone.

Two patterns explain the table. First, in every surviving option for famous recordings the app never copies or transmits the music, so **what decides the option is each service's contract with developers and what a phone browser lets a web page do**, not a copyright licence. The two strictest contracts, Spotify's and Apple's, both name synchronisation; options in which the app never touches a service (own files, tap-along) have no such contract to break. Second, no service supplies a beat grid. Spotify closed its beat data in 2024, Apple's catalogue has no tempo field, and TIDAL and SoundCloud give at most a single tempo number per song, so the grid is always the developer's own work ([Apple Music API](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/applemusicapi/songs/attributes-data.dictionary); [TIDAL API types](https://github.com/tidal-music/tidal-sdk-web/blob/main/packages/api/src/allAPI.generated.ts)).

## Spotify is closed on every route, the virtual browser included

None of Spotify's own pages could be opened during the research. The Spotify quotations here are search-result excerpts of the linked pages; two researchers and press coverage agree on their substance.

A "virtual browser" can mean two things, and both fail. Placing Spotify's own web player inside the app's page is refused by Spotify's servers, and a page cannot override the refusal ([Custom iFrame docs](https://docs.customiframe.com/troubleshooting/embed-blocked)). Renting a browser on a server, logging it in to the dancer's account and showing it to the dancer as a live stream is sold as a service, but it fails on four counts at once. Spotify's user rules prohibit crawling or scraping the service "by manual or automated means, including bots, scrapers, and spiders", "circumventing any technology used by Spotify", and "copying, reproducing, redistributing, 'ripping,' recording, transferring, performing" its content ([Spotify User Guidelines](https://www.spotify.com/us/legal/user-guidelines/)). The developer's server would receive licensed music and pass it on, the pattern for which YouTube closed the Discord music bots Groovy and Rythm by legal letter in 2021 ([9to5Google](https://9to5google.com/2021/09/13/youtube-nukes-popular-groovy-bot-discord-music-service-for-breaking-platforms-tos/)). The music would arrive through a video-call-style stream with a varying delay, which makes beat-accurate footwork harder. And the one price found is US$0.007 per participant-minute after a free allowance, about **US$0.42 per dancer per hour**, so a thousand dancers practising two hours a month would cost roughly US$770 a month ([Hyperbeam](https://hyperbeam.com/)). Dancers would also be typing their Spotify password into a browser the developer controls. The idea should be dropped.

Spotify does offer official ways to do what the question describes: a player that runs inside a web page (the Web Playback SDK) and a remote control for the Spotify app already running on the dancer's phone or computer (the Web API). Both are shut to this app. Since March 2026 an individual developer's app works for **at most five named Spotify users**, and the developer must hold Premium; the unlimited tier is open only to registered organisations with at least 250,000 monthly users ([Spotify quota modes](https://developer.spotify.com/documentation/web-api/concepts/quota-modes); [Spotify, 6 Feb 2026](https://developer.spotify.com/blog/2026-02-06-update-on-developer-access-and-platform-security); [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2026/02/06/spotify-changes-developer-mode-api-to-require-premium-accounts-limits-test-users/)). An independent developer whose app fell under a prohibited category asked for more users and was told "there isn't a workaround or exception available" ([Spotify Community](https://community.spotify.com/t5/Spotify-for-Developers/Extended-Quota-Mode-Request-Independent-Developer-TrackLine/td-p/7384615)). Beyond the cap, Spotify's developer rules read like a list of this app's features: "Do not synchronize any sound recordings with any visual media", and do not let any system "segue, mix, re-mix, or overlap any Spotify Content with any other audio content" ([Spotify Developer Policy](https://developer.spotify.com/policy)). The platform also "can not be used to develop applications that alter Spotify Content", which covers a tempo change, and commercial use of an app that plays Spotify music is not permitted; no Spotify statement was found on whether a donation button counts ([Spotify Web API reference](https://developer.spotify.com/documentation/web-api/reference/get-the-users-currently-playing-track); [Spotify Community](https://community.spotify.com/t5/Spotify-for-Developers/Do-I-have-to-wait-for-commercial-app-approval/td-p/5100576)).

The tools would not meet the app's needs even without those rules. Neither can change tempo ([Spotify Community](https://community.spotify.com/t5/Spotify-for-Developers/Is-a-speed-method-on-the-roadmap-for-the-Web-Playback-SDK/td-p/5072771)), the remote control reports a position that developers describe as "always randomly delayed by 0.5-1.5s" ([Spotify Community](https://community.spotify.com/t5/Spotify-for-Developers/Web-API-precise-playback-progress/td-p/6130785)), and Spotify closed its beat-and-bar data to new apps on 27 November 2024 ([Spotify, 27 Nov 2024](https://developer.spotify.com/blog/2024-11-27-changes-to-the-web-api)). The one Spotify door without a user cap is the small embedded widget that sites use to show a track. A page can start, pause and seek it, but it plays full songs only when the visitor is logged in to Spotify in that browser, one developer reports 30-second previews on phones and in Safari even then, it has no speed control, and Spotify's widget terms forbid covering or altering it ([Spotify iFrame API](https://developer.spotify.com/documentation/embeds/references/iframe-api); [Spotify Community](https://community.spotify.com/t5/Other-Podcasts-Partners-etc/Spotify-Play-Button-30-second-limit/td-p/4552931); [developer report](https://github.com/TheKaoser/classical-portal/pull/88); [Spotify Widget Terms](https://developer.spotify.com/documentation/embeds/terms)). History points the same way: Spotify cut off every third-party DJ app on 1 July 2020 and the slow-down practice apps on 1 September 2022, and when mixing and tempo control returned in September 2025 it was only inside named commercial DJ products ([Engadget](https://www.engadget.com/2020/03/20/spotify-ends-support-third-party-dj-apps/); [Spotify Community](https://community.spotify.com/t5/Spotify-for-Developers/Please-continue-supporting-iOS-Android-streaming-SDKs/td-p/5416477/page/24); [Spotify Newsroom](https://newsroom.spotify.com/2025-09-24/dj-software-integration-premium/)). What remains for Spotify listeners is the tap-along mode described below, in which the dancer plays the song in their own Spotify app and the web app never touches Spotify.

## Among streaming services, only YouTube's visible player is open to this app

### No account, no fee, no user cap, and the controls the app needs

YouTube lets any site place its player on a page and steer it with the site's own buttons. This needs no key, no registration and no sign-in by the visitor ([YouTube Developer Policies](https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/developer-policies)), and the documented controls cover play, pause, seek, volume, speed and reading the position ([YouTube player reference](https://developers.google.com/youtube/iframe_api_reference)). Speed is better than Google's documentation suggests: two current sources report that the player accepts **any speed from 25% to 200% in steps of 5%**, so the app's range of roughly 70% to 115% is available as ten steps, and browsers keep the pitch steady by default ([Video.js YouTube adapter, Nov 2026](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@videojs/youtube-video); [Soundslice help](https://www.soundslice.com/help/en/player/basic/5/playback-speed/); [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/docs/Web/API/HTMLMediaElement/preservesPitch)). The pattern has the longest track record found. Soundslice has synchronised music notation with YouTube videos since 2012 and Chordify chords since 2013, and Soundslice plays its own metronome "on top of the audio" of YouTube recordings ([Holovaty](https://holovaty.com/writing/soundslice); [Chordify](https://chordify.net/pages/about/); [Soundslice help](https://www.soundslice.com/help/en/player/basic/8/metronome/)). No report was found of YouTube acting against a product of this kind, which is an absence of reports, not an approval. Every enforcement case found involved sound taken out of the visible player or played without it: the Streamus extension in 2015, the Discord bots in 2021, the Musi app in 2024 ([Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2015/07/21/streamus-youtube-music-chrome-extension-takedown/); [Music Business Worldwide](https://www.musicbusinessworldwide.com/free-streaming-app-musi-sues-apple-over-removal-from-app-store/)).

### The price: a visible window, coarse timing, ads and iPhone limits

The conditions are firm. The player must stay on screen at **200 by 200 pixels or more** with nothing drawn over it, more than half of it must be visible when the app starts a song, ads must be left alone, and playback must not be charged for or gated ([YouTube Required Minimum Functionality](https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/required-minimum-functionality); [YouTube Developer Policies](https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/developer-policies)). The site must link to YouTube's terms of service and have its users agree to a privacy policy. One clause is a grey area: developers must not "separate, isolate, or modify the audio or video components" of a video, with the example "you must not apply alternate audio tracks to videos" ([YouTube Developer Policies](https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/developer-policies)). The clause does not mention sounds that a page plays alongside, and Soundslice does this openly, but a strict reader could turn it against a constant click and voice. Those sounds should stay optional and switchable, as the drums and voice already are in the Android app. No retrieved clause mentions donation buttons; the safe design keeps the donation link on its own page, where the Android app's heart page already puts it. YouTube's terms cover personal, non-commercial viewing, so a teacher playing songs from the app to a class is outside them, and the app is safer presented as a step trainer than as a music player ([YouTube Terms of Service](https://www.youtube.com/t/terms)).

Timing is the main technical risk. The player offers no clock: the app must ask for the position many times a second, and a developer who built a rhythm game this way found that the player "gives you a video player, not a clock" and that the reported time "is coarse. It updates in chunks and freezes while buffering" ([DEV Community](https://dev.to/leviyi/syncing-a-rhythm-game-to-a-youtube-iframe-a-metronome-on-someone-elses-player-3la0)). Soundslice says of its own metronome that "for YouTube, it's a bit trickier and is still on our to-do list" ([Soundslice blog](https://www.soundslice.com/blog/282/new-features-dec-12/)). Jumps do not always land where asked, so the app has to read back where the music actually resumed ([YouTube player parameters](https://developers.google.com/youtube/player_parameters)). The remedy is known: smooth the reported position with the app's own clock and give the dancer an "earlier / later" adjustment per device. No measurement exists for phones, which is why a test comes first.

On iPhone three further limits apply. The player must already be loaded before the dancer taps Play ([lite-youtube-embed](https://github.com/paulirish/lite-youtube-embed)). A web page cannot change a player's volume there, so the music can be muted but not lowered under the voice ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/docs/Web/API/HTMLMediaElement/volume)). And the ring/silent switch silences the app's own clicks and voice while the video keeps sounding ([unmute-ios-audio](https://github.com/feross/unmute-ios-audio)). A developer reports that on Android the music pauses as soon as the page is hidden; the same is expected, though not sourced, when an iPhone locks. YouTube forbids features that play "from a background player", so the app should keep the screen awake while a song plays instead of working around the pause ([developer report](https://github.com/DhruvHarani1/vega/issues/5); [YouTube Developer Policies](https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/developer-policies); [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/docs/Web/API/WakeLock)).

Each song is one specific video, and videos are fragile. Ads cannot be switched off or predicted, and blocking them is prohibited ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/132596?hl=en)). Owners and rights claims can stop a video from playing on other sites, and YouTube states that its own "embeddable" flag does not settle the matter ([YouTube Data API](https://developers.google.com/youtube/v3/docs/videos)), so every chosen video has to be tried on the real site and re-checked over time; no figures were found on how often swing recordings are blocked. YouTube's automatically generated "Topic" videos carry the label's own audio file, which suits a beat grid, but it is not known how often they play on other sites ([Symphonic](https://support.symdistro.com/hc/en-us/articles/115000437283-YouTube-Art-Tracks)). A beat grid belongs to one upload, because another upload of the same recording starts at a different moment. The grid must be made without downloading YouTube's sound, which YouTube's terms prohibit: by tapping the beats against the player, or by analysing a copy the developer bought and lining it up by ear. YouTube Music offers no separate door; its songs are ordinary YouTube videos ([ytmusicapi](https://pypi.org/project/ytmusicapi/)).

### Apple Music works on paper, but its licence forbids synchronising

Apple Music is the only other mainstream service an individual can sign up to alone. Membership of the Apple Developer Program costs US$99 a year, there is no user cap, and Apple's web player (MusicKit on the Web) plays full songs for signed-in subscribers inside the app's page, with play, pause, seek and a speed setting that an open-source Apple Music app drives from 25% to 200% ([Apple Developer Program](https://developer.apple.com/programs/); [Apple MusicKit](https://developer.apple.com/musickit/); [Cider source](https://github.com/ciderapp/Cider/blob/main/src/renderer/views/components/audio-playbackrate.ejs)). Three things hold it back. First, the licence, updated on 29 October 2026, says developers may not "modify any MusicKit Content", that it **"cannot be synchronized with any other content, unless otherwise permitted by Apple in the Documentation"**, and that they may not "require payment for or indirectly monetize access to the Apple Music service" ([Apple Developer Program License Agreement](https://developer.apple.com/support/terms/apple-developer-program-license-agreement/)). Apple's engineers decline to rule on designs ("I can't tell you whether a particular design meets those terms"), and a 2024 question from a choreography-teaching app has no reply ([Apple forum 852793](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/852793); [Apple forum 764676](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/764676)). Second, Apple still labels the web player "Beta", names no supported browsers, and has open sign-in failures in November 2026 ([Apple forum 837028](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/837028)). Third, on Apple's own browser engine a speed change "appears to interrupt playback briefly", and iPhone volume cannot be changed from a page ([Apple forum 829729](https://developer.apple.com/forums/thread/829729); [Apple Safari guide](https://developer.apple.com/library/archive/documentation/AudioVideo/Conceptual/Using_HTML5_Audio_Video/Device-SpecificConsiderations/Device-SpecificConsiderations.html)). The precedents are real but narrower than they look: independent iPhone apps such as Perfect Tempo have slowed Apple Music tracks for years, as installed apps and without synchronised pictures ([Perfect Tempo](https://openplanetsoftware.com/perfect-tempo)). The sensible course is to send Apple the exact design and build nothing on it until a written answer arrives.

### The remaining services are closed or carry the wrong music

TIDAL publishes a capable web player, but paying subscribers who use outside apps still receive 30-second previews, and TIDAL has given no firm date for full songs ([TIDAL developer discussion](https://github.com/orgs/tidal-music/discussions/351)). Deezer's web tools are unsupported and it "has no sign-in for outside apps"; Amazon Music's web interface is a closed test programme for approved partners ([Deezer samples](https://github.com/deezer/javascript-samples/issues/8); [Music Assistant](https://github.com/music-assistant/music-assistant.io/blob/main/src/content/docs/music-providers/deezer.md); [Amazon Music](https://www.developer.amazon.com/docs/music/API_web_overview.html)). SoundCloud's free widget can be steered from a page but has no speed control, and its subscription-only tracks play as 30-second snippets unless the listener signs in inside the widget; SoundCloud says its label contracts keep those tracks out of its public interface ([SoundCloud issue 622](https://github.com/soundcloud/api/issues/622); [SoundCloud issue 567](https://github.com/soundcloud/api/issues/567)). Audius hands a page a plain music file with full control, free of charge, but holds independent uploads, not the swing canon ([Audius SDK](https://github.com/AudiusProject/apps/blob/main/packages/sdk/README.md)). Paid "music for apps" suppliers such as Feed.fm price by quote, play radio-style without song choice, and do not support phone browsers ([Feed.fm SDK](https://github.com/feedfm/Javascript-SDK)).

## The dancer's own files deliver every feature; tapping along covers every other app

When the dancer opens a music file from their own device, the app holds the sound itself and every requirement falls into place. **Music, metronome and voice run on one clock**, so they stay exactly together. The song can be slowed or sped with a free, openly licensed component (Signalsmith Stretch) whose makers say it "sounds best" between 75% and 150%, which puts the app's slowest setting of about 70% just outside the comfortable range ([Signalsmith Stretch](https://www.npmjs.com/package/signalsmith-stretch); [Signalsmith README](https://github.com/Signalsmith-Audio/signalsmith-stretch/blob/main/README.md)). Because the sound passes through the app's own mixer, the music can be lowered under the voice on iPhone as well. The simpler alternative, the browser's built-in player with its own speed setting, leaves music and clicks on two clocks, and on iPhones that predate the September 2026 Safari release its reported position advanced only in steps ([Safari 27 release notes](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/safari-release-notes/safari-27-release-notes)). Nothing is uploaded or distributed, no service's rules apply, and tools of this kind, from the practice app Anytune to the swing DJ player LynDJ, show no record of being cut off ([Anytune help](https://anytune.zendesk.com/hc/en-us/articles/360015557892); [LynDJ](https://github.com/Ghostkeeper/LynDJ)). The same sound engine plays the app's built-in tracks, so it has to be built anyway. What still needs care is the screen. Sound leaves a phone late, by a few milliseconds on an iPhone and by up to 150 ms on the cheapest Android phones according to older engineering notes, with more buffering in Bluetooth headsets, so the animation needs the delay the browser reports plus a manual slider, which is what rhythm games give their players ([Web Audio performance notes](https://padenot.github.io/web-audio-perf/); [Oboe Bluetooth note](https://github.com/google/oboe/wiki/TechNote_BluetoothAudio); [osu! wiki](https://github.com/ppy/osu-wiki/blob/master/wiki/Offset/Universal_offset/en.md)).

The limits concern people and phones, not rules. Few listeners still own files: streaming made up 69.6% of recorded-music revenue in 2025 and download sales fell for the thirteenth year running, although swing DJs still work from files ([Music Business Worldwide](https://musicbusinessworldwide.com/10-quick-and-crucial-takeaways-from-ifpis-global-music-report-2026)). On iPhone the browser picks files only from the Files app, not from the Music library, and songs downloaded from a subscription are copy-protected and cannot be opened at all ([WebKit source](https://github.com/WebKit/WebKit/blob/main/Source/WebKit/UIProcess/ios/forms/WKFileUploadPanel.mm); [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/mediaplayer/mpmediaitem/hasprotectedasset)). Remembering a song between visits is easy only in Chrome. Safari deletes a site's stored data after seven days of browser use without a visit, so the app should store the small beat grid and ask for the file again ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Storage_API/Storage_quotas_and_eviction_criteria)).

The beat grid is the real work. For songs on the app's curated list, the browser can recognise the dancer's file by a compact fingerprint and line it up with the stored grid to about a hundredth of a second, with no audio shipped ([Chromaprint for browsers](https://www.npmjs.com/package/@unimusic/chromaprint); [BBC audio-offset-finder](https://github.com/bbc/audio-offset-finder/blob/master/README.md)). For unknown songs an automatic beat finder can run in the browser (Beat This!, free to use, about 25 MB of downloads), but it produces a draft. On a jazz test set, automatic analysis scored 0.97 out of 1 for finding beats and only 0.63 for finding the first beat of the bar, and general detectors halve or double fast swing tempos ([Beat This!](https://github.com/CPJKU/beat_this/blob/main/README.md); [Jazz Trio Database](https://transactions.ismir.net/articles/10.5334/tismir.186); [swing-bpm](https://pypi.org/project/swing-bpm/)). DJ software treats automatic analysis the same way, as a first draft that a person corrects by tapping and shifting ([Mixxx manual](https://github.com/mixxxdj/manual/blob/main/source/chapters/user_interface.rst)). The app therefore needs a small editor ("tap on 1", shift, half or double tempo), and that editor is also the heart of the tap-along mode.

Tap-along serves the dancer whose music lives in Spotify, Apple Music or anywhere else: they start the song in that app, tap the beat in the web app, and the feet follow. It works on every device, with headphones and with no permission, and on iPhone a page that makes only its own sounds is treated like a play-along instrument that mixes over the other app's music; this follows from Apple's documentation and was not tested ([Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfaudio/avaudiosession/category-swift.struct/ambient)). It cannot start, pause, seek or slow the song, and it drifts. By one researcher's estimate, not a measurement, sixteen taps at 160 beats a minute keep the feet within 50 ms for only about fourteen seconds, and live bands wander, so the screen needs one large "1" button to re-anchor and small nudge buttons. A web page has no way to ask the phone what another app is playing ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Media_Session_API)). Listening through the microphone is weaker than it sounds. On an iPhone, Safari's listening interrupts music playing on the same phone, so a second device or speaker is needed, and the best live beat-follower in one comparison scored about 75 out of 100 on beats and 46 on the first beat of the bar ([WebKit source](https://github.com/WebKit/WebKit/blob/main/Source/WebCore/platform/audio/cocoa/MediaSessionManagerCocoa.mm); [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/avfaudio/avaudiosession/category-swift.struct/playandrecord); [beat-tracking comparison](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.00704)). Recognising a known recording and locking a prepared grid to it, as Shazam does for lyrics, is the stronger variant, but the open-source matcher found comes with patent warnings and a licence that obliges any app built on it to publish its own code on the same terms ([Olaf](https://github.com/JorenSix/Olaf/blob/master/README.md)). Capturing a browser tab's sound works only in desktop Chrome and Edge ([MDN compatibility data](https://github.com/mdn/browser-compat-data/blob/main/api/MediaDevices.json)). Both are later projects.

## The app can ship credited free-licence tracks, a band's permission and commissioned swing

Built-in music stays the default that always works, and it needs one repair now. The Android app ships "Hot Swing" by Kevin MacLeod under CC BY 4.0 and two tracks by a real big band under CC BY-NC-SA 4.0, each in seven tempos, but its screens show only the track title, and its About line credits the font and the icons, not the music ([project README](../README.md); [app strings](../app/src/main/res/values/strings.xml)). Both licences require the creator's name, a notice of the licence with a link, and a note of changes wherever the music is shared, and rights lost through a lapse return automatically if it is fixed within 30 days of being noticed ([CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/legalcode.en)). A credits screen settles "Hot Swing" for every version of the app, the web version included.

The two band tracks carry three doubts that no authority settles. "NonCommercial" means "not primarily intended for or directed towards commercial advantage or monetary compensation", and Creative Commons itself says it "cannot advise you on what is and is not commercial use", so a donation button is probably, but not safely, acceptable. The licence treats music "synched in timed relation with a moving image" as an adaptation, which pulls its share-alike condition (adaptations must carry the same licence) towards the app's animation. And app-store packaging might count as a forbidden technical restriction ([CC BY-NC-SA 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-nc-sa/4.0/legalcode.en); [CC FAQ](https://creativecommons.org/faq/#does-my-use-violate-the-noncommercial-clause-of-the-licenses)). The licence lets the band grant separate terms, so **one short written permission from the band removes all three doubts**, and the project's own to-do list already calls for it ([project TODO](../TODO.md)). The letter should also ask who wrote the two tunes and whether the writers belong to a collecting society, a body that collects royalties on songwriters' behalf. Until it arrives, the band tracks do not belong in a release with a live donation link.

Shipping the famous recordings is out for a worldwide app. United States law protects recordings first published from 1923 to 1946 for 100 years, so sides issued in 1935 to 1945 become free there between **1 January 2036 and 2046**. In the EU and UK every recording published by 1962 is already free as a recording, but the tune is a second layer, protected until 70 years after its last writer's death ([17 U.S.C. § 1401, mirror](https://github.com/lexifina/united-states-law/blob/main/title-17/1401.md); [Directive 2006/116/EC Art. 10, mirror](https://github.com/lexifina/european-union-law/blob/main/copyright-term-directive/art-010.md); [Art. 1, mirror](https://github.com/lexifina/european-union-law/blob/main/copyright-term-directive/art-001.md)). Licensing them is built for funded companies. Suppliers of music for apps quote monthly fees, and Peloton's use of songs without publishers' licences drew a claim of more than US$300 million before it settled in 2020 ([Feed.fm pricing](https://www.feed.fm/pricing-page); [Music Business Worldwide](https://musicbusinessworldwide.com/music-publishers-and-peloton-reach-settlement-over-copyright-infringement-lawsuit)).

More good swing therefore has to come from small rights holders. The project's own sweep of the free-licence libraries in October 2026 found a handful of freely usable tracks, mostly computer-sequenced, and real bands only under non-commercial or no-changes terms ([project music notes](../music/README.md)). A direct, one-page licence from an independent swing band fits best, and is simplest when the band wrote the tunes itself. Commissioning recordings of out-of-copyright or new tunes at chosen tempos is the only route to real-band swing with no conditions left, provided every writer, arranger and player signs their rights over; no cost figures could be verified. Stock-music libraries and AI services are unverified reserves. No library's licence or price page could be opened, and although one AI service's terms give users ownership of what they generate "to the extent permitted by applicable law", the owner's earlier reaction to machine-made swing was negative ([Stability AI terms](https://github.com/OpenTermsArchive/GenAI-versions/blob/main/Stability%20AI/Terms%20of%20Service.md); [project music notes](../music/README.md)).

## Where the notes disagree, and what nobody could check

The six research notes agree on the main conclusions. They differ on the points below, mostly because one researcher found something another could not.

| Question | One note says | Another note says | How this report settles it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Which speeds does YouTube's player allow? | The precedents note: "a fixed set of speeds" | The YouTube note: any speed from 25% to 200% in 5% steps, per the November 2026 code of a widely used player library (Video.js) and Soundslice's help; Google's own reference lists only quarter steps | The 5% steps are real today but undocumented by Google. Offer 70% to 115% in 5% steps, show the speed the player reports back, and test on phones |
| Is there a precedent, or a rule, for the app's own sounds over YouTube music? | The precedents note: no precedent and no rule found | The YouTube note: Soundslice's metronome over YouTube is a documented feature, and a policy clause bans modifying a video's audio or adding "alternate audio tracks" | A precedent exists and the clause does not name added sounds. The risk is moderate and unsettled; keep the sounds optional |
| Is Apple Music friendly to this kind of app? | The precedents note: the one service where small apps have slowed subscription tracks for years | The Apple note: the licence forbids synchronising its music with other content, and Apple will not interpret it | Both are true. Speed change is tolerated in installed iPhone apps; a synchronised animation contradicts the plain wording |
| How many services allow tempo change on mainstream recordings in an iPhone browser? | The Apple note: exactly one, Apple Music | The YouTube note: YouTube's player does too | The Apple researcher's brief excluded YouTube. There are two |
| How many Spotify apps may one developer register? | The precedents note: one (February 2026) | The Spotify note: 25 since 23 July 2026, sharing one usage allowance | 25 apps, still five users each; spreading users across them would dodge an explicit limit |
| Does Spotify's widget play full songs? | A forum statement: only for Premium users logged in on a desktop browser | Third-party guides: for any logged-in user; a developer: previews on phones and in Safari | Unsettled. No current official statement was found and nobody tested it |
| Can the app follow music by listening? | The precedents note: Shazam-style recognition is the strongest precedent | The own-files note: on an iPhone, Safari cannot listen while another app on the same phone plays | Proven in installed apps. In a web page it needs Android or a second device |
| What does a stock track cost? | The project's earlier plan: US$20 to 50 per track, with a licence that covers apps | The licensing note: no source for that, and probably wrong for subscription libraries | Unverified. Read the licence before buying |

Smaller differences change no conclusion. The notes place Spotify's ban on overlapping audio in two different documents (its Developer Policy and its Developer Terms), and neither page was opened. Press reports give looser dates for Spotify's rule changes than Spotify's own posts, which say 15 May 2025 for the organisations-only rule and 11 February and 9 March 2026 for the five-user cap on new and existing apps. And whether Apple's current documentation presents the web player's speed as something a page may set, which matters under the licence's "unless otherwise permitted" wording, is unconfirmed: a working app sets it, an older community description calls it read-only, and Apple's current reference could not be opened.

The evidence falls into three grades. **Read directly, and therefore firmest:** Apple's licence, review guidelines, documentation and developer forum; the published code and discussion boards of TIDAL, SoundCloud, Audius, Deezer's samples, WebKit and Chromium; the software packages quoted for YouTube's speed steps and iPhone behaviour; browser-support data; the full text of the two Creative Commons licences; and the project's own files. The copyright statutes were read in mirror copies, not on official sites. **Seen only as search-result excerpts, never on the page itself:** every Spotify page (developer terms and policy, user guidelines, widget terms, the five-user and organisations-only rules, the forum threads); every YouTube and Google policy page (the 200-pixel rule, the overlay and audio clauses, the terms of service), whose list of player controls was read instead in software packages that copy Google's reference; Soundslice's help pages; the Creative Commons FAQ; Amazon Music's statements; Hyperbeam's price; the press reports and most product pages cited as precedents; and the accuracy figures for beat finders and the music-industry revenue figures. These excerpts are very probably accurate, but the exact wording and the surrounding sentences were not seen, and the claim that Spotify blocks its web player from other pages rests on one third-party table. **From a researcher's memory only, and therefore left out of the findings above:** all prices for stock music, collecting-society licences, session musicians and AI services; the current terms of Suno and Udio; Israel's copyright terms; the dates of individual tunes and records; and the terms of Qobuz, Bandcamp, Jamendo and Beatport.

Several decisive facts were found by nobody: how closely YouTube's reported position tracks the sound on phones; whether the 5% speed steps work in iPhone Safari and Android Chrome; how often swing recordings are blocked from playing on other sites, or removed; whether Apple's web player plays full songs reliably in phone browsers; how truthfully phones report Bluetooth delay; and what any service thinks of a donation button, on which every rulebook examined is silent. The shared search budget ran out part-way, so planned look-ups on YouTube-based dance-practice sites and web DJ apps never ran, and none of the researchers tested anything on a device. This report is research, not legal advice. Every citation is a link, so the wording can be checked in an ordinary browser; Spotify's Developer Policy and YouTube's Developer Policies are the two pages worth reading first.

## Conclusion

The research changes the shape of the question. It began as a search for a way into Spotify's catalogue; it ends with the finding that the app's lasting asset is its own beat grids, footwork and voice, and that the sound source should be a replaceable part. Spotify's record shows why: four withdrawals in six years, each by whole category and through no fault of the developers affected. The routes that last use a feature offered to every visitor, such as YouTube's ordinary player and its speed menu, or need no service at all. The swing world's own tools either make their own sound or play the dancer's files, and in limited searching none was found that animates steps to a real recording, so the niche is open.

The next step is a test, not a build. A bare page with a YouTube player and a flashing beat, tried on one iPhone and one Android phone with a few recordings from the intended list, will show whether the feet stay within about 50 ms of the music, whether the 5% speeds work, and whether those videos play on another site at all. If it passes, YouTube mode earns its place beside built-in tracks and own files. If it fails, YouTube becomes a loose play-along source, and the weight shifts to own files and to commissioning a few real-band tracks, released under a free licence so that other dancers' tools can use them too. Two things are worth watching meanwhile: TIDAL's promised opening of full songs to outside apps, which would be the best technical fit of any streaming service, and Apple's answer to a direct question about synchronised animation.
